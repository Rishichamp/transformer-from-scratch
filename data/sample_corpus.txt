Notes on Attention and Memory

A neural network learns by adjusting numbers. At the start of training, every weight is a small random value, and the model knows nothing at all. As examples pass through it again and again, the weights shift in tiny steps, guided by the gradient of a loss function. Over many thousands of steps, these tiny shifts accumulate into something that looks like understanding.

The attention mechanism solves a simple but important problem. When a model reads a sentence, not every word matters equally for predicting the next word. If the sentence is "The trophy did not fit in the suitcase because it was too big", the word "it" needs to connect back to "trophy", not "suitcase". A model with attention can learn to make exactly this connection, by assigning a high weight to the relevant earlier word and a low weight to everything else.

Before attention became popular, recurrent networks processed text one token at a time, carrying a single hidden state forward like a runner passing a baton. The problem with a single hidden state is that it has to compress everything important from the past into a fixed-size vector. As the sentence gets longer, older information gets crowded out by newer information. This is the vanishing gradient problem in practice: information from early tokens has less and less influence on the final prediction.

Attention removes this bottleneck by letting every token look directly at every other token, no matter how far apart they are. The cost is that comparing every pair of tokens takes time proportional to the square of the sequence length. For short sequences this is fine. For very long documents, this quadratic cost becomes the main obstacle, which is why so much research effort has gone into making attention cheaper for long contexts.

A transformer block has two main parts: the attention layer and the feed-forward layer. The attention layer mixes information between positions. The feed-forward layer processes each position independently, applying the same transformation to every token. Together, these two parts are repeated many times, stacked on top of each other, with the output of one block feeding into the next.

Positional encoding solves a different problem. Attention by itself treats the input as a bag of tokens with no order, because the same dot product happens between any two positions regardless of where they sit in the sequence. Without some signal about position, the model cannot tell "the dog bit the man" from "the man bit the dog", since both sentences contain the same set of tokens.

The original transformer paper added a fixed pattern of sine and cosine waves to each token's embedding, with a different frequency for each dimension. Lower dimensions oscillate slowly across the sequence, capturing coarse position information, while higher dimensions oscillate quickly, capturing fine position information. This pattern is fixed in advance and never changes during training.

A later idea, often called rotary position embedding, takes a different approach. Instead of adding a position signal to the embedding, it rotates the query and key vectors inside the attention calculation by an angle that depends on position. The clever part is that after this rotation, the dot product between a query at position m and a key at position n depends only on the difference m minus n, not on the absolute positions themselves. This means the model naturally learns about relative distances between tokens, which tends to generalise better to sequences longer than anything seen during training.

Another approach skips rotation entirely and instead adds a penalty to the attention score based on how far apart two tokens are. The penalty grows linearly with distance, and different attention heads use different penalty strengths. Heads with a steep penalty end up focusing on nearby tokens, almost like a local window. Heads with a gentle penalty can still attend across the whole sequence when needed. This gives the model a mix of local and global attention patterns without any extra parameters to learn.

Training a language model is, at its core, a single repeated task: given some tokens, predict the next one. The loss function measures how surprised the model is by the actual next token, on average, across a batch of examples. A model that assigns high probability to the correct token has low loss. A model that is confidently wrong has very high loss. Over time, the model learns to be appropriately uncertain: spreading probability across several plausible continuations when the context genuinely allows for multiple outcomes, and concentrating probability sharply when the context strongly determines what comes next.

Gradient clipping is a small but important safeguard during training. Occasionally, a particular batch produces an unusually large gradient, perhaps because of a rare token or an unlucky combination of examples. Without clipping, this single large gradient could push the weights to a bad region of the loss landscape, undoing much of the progress made so far. Clipping simply caps the size of the gradient before it is applied, so no single step can be too disruptive.

Learning rate scheduling shapes how quickly the model adapts at different points in training. At the very start, the weights are random and the gradients can be large and noisy, so a small learning rate during this warmup phase prevents early instability. As training progresses and the model settles into a more useful region of the loss landscape, the learning rate is gradually reduced, often following a smooth curve, allowing the model to fine-tune its behaviour with smaller and smaller adjustments.

Layer normalisation stabilises training by rescaling the activations at each layer so they have a consistent mean and spread, regardless of how large or small the inputs happen to be. This matters because activations can otherwise grow or shrink unpredictably as they pass through many layers, making training unstable. By normalising at each step, every layer receives inputs in a similar range, which makes the optimisation problem much easier.

Dropout is a regularisation technique that randomly disables a fraction of activations during training. The idea is to prevent the network from relying too heavily on any single pathway or feature. If a particular connection is sometimes missing, the network is forced to develop multiple redundant ways of representing useful information, which tends to generalise better to new examples it has not seen before.

When generating text, a model produces a probability distribution over its entire vocabulary for the next token, given everything generated so far. The simplest strategy is to always pick the single most likely token, but this tends to produce repetitive, predictable text. Sampling introduces controlled randomness: instead of always picking the top choice, the model picks according to the probabilities themselves, so less likely but still plausible continuations get a chance to appear. Temperature adjusts how sharply peaked or flat this distribution is before sampling, while nucleus sampling restricts the choice to only the most probable tokens that together account for most of the probability mass, avoiding the long tail of very unlikely options.
